The History and Science of Cartography

The History and Science of Cartography

For most of human history, the act of drawing a map was an act of profound ambition. To represent the world on a flat surface requires not only skill and observation but a willingness to confront the limits of what is known, to mark the edges of the known with the honest admission that beyond them lies territory that has not yet been seen and may not yet be understood. The earliest maps we have recovered from archaeological sites are not maps of the whole world, or even of large regions, but of specific and local things: the fields around a settlement, the course of a river between two villages, the layout of a city as its planners imagined it ought to be built. These local maps were not attempts to describe the world as it was but to organise a small piece of it in a way that made collective action possible. They were tools of cooperation before they were instruments of knowledge.

The transition from local mapping to cosmographical mapping, from the representation of known places to the representation of the entire globe, happened gradually and unevenly across different civilisations. In ancient Mesopotamia, scribes produced clay tablets that depicted the known world as a flat disc surrounded by ocean, with Babylon positioned at the centre. This was not naivety about the shape of the earth but rather a deliberate statement about the importance of Babylon relative to everything else. Maps have always carried ideological weight as well as geographical information, and the choice of what to place at the centre of a map is never a purely technical decision. It reflects the priorities and self-understanding of the culture that produced it, and this remains as true of modern cartography as it was of ancient Mesopotamian clay tablets.

Greek geographers made significant advances in the theoretical foundations of cartography, most notably through the work of Claudius Ptolemy in the second century, whose Geography provided a systematic framework for representing the curved surface of the earth on a flat plane. Ptolemy understood that any such projection would involve distortion, that it was mathematically impossible to faithfully represent a sphere on a flat surface without sacrificing accuracy in some dimension, and he proposed several different projection methods as solutions to this problem, each making different trade-offs between the accurate representation of shape, area, distance, and direction. The projections Ptolemy described remained foundational to European cartography for more than a thousand years, and even after his specific geographical errors had been corrected by later travellers and surveyors, his mathematical framework continued to structure the way that western cartographers thought about their work.

Medieval European maps took a different approach, one that prioritised theological and symbolic organisation over geographical accuracy. The so-called mappae mundi, of which several hundred survive in various states of completeness, placed Jerusalem at the centre of the known world and organised the continents of Europe, Asia, and Africa around it in ways that reflected biblical cosmology rather than measured observation. These maps were not intended for navigation. They were intended for contemplation, for the education of viewers in the Christian understanding of history and geography, for the visualisation of pilgrimage routes and sacred sites. Judging them by the standards of modern cartography misunderstands their purpose entirely. They were not bad maps of the physical world. They were excellent maps of a theological one.

The period of European maritime expansion in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries transformed cartography in ways that are difficult to overstate. The Portuguese and Spanish voyages of exploration generated a flood of new geographical information that existing maps could not accommodate, and the practical requirements of oceanic navigation created urgent demand for maps that were accurate in ways that medieval mappae mundi had never needed to be. The portolan charts that had been developed for Mediterranean navigation, with their dense networks of compass lines and their careful representation of coastlines, were extended and adapted for Atlantic and then global use. Gerardus Mercator's projection of 1569, which represented lines of constant compass bearing as straight lines, became the dominant framework for navigational cartography for the following four centuries, a dominance that persisted long after oceanic navigation had ceased to be the primary use case for most maps, and which produced in the popular imagination a distorted picture of the world, one in which Greenland appears as large as Africa and Europe appears larger than South America, when in fact both of these comparisons are dramatically wrong.

The political dimensions of cartography became increasingly explicit during the colonial period. European powers used maps not only to record territories they had explored but to assert claims over territories they intended to acquire, to define borders that suited their strategic interests rather than the social and political realities of the peoples who actually lived within them, and to render indigenous geographical knowledge invisible by replacing it with European naming conventions and European frameworks of organisation. The straight-line borders that cut across Africa, the Middle East, and South Asia are the most visible legacy of this process, borders that were drawn by European diplomats and cartographers with reference to astronomical coordinates and strategic considerations rather than to the ethnic, linguistic, or ecological patterns that structured life on the ground. The consequences of these decisions continue to shape geopolitical conflict in the twenty-first century.

The development of aerial photography in the twentieth century transformed cartographic practice by making it possible to produce accurate maps of large areas far more quickly and cheaply than ground-based survey methods allowed. Photogrammetry, the extraction of geometric information from photographs, became a central technique in professional cartography, and the maps produced by aerial survey were measurably more accurate in their representation of terrain and land cover than anything that had come before. The addition of satellite imagery in the latter half of the twentieth century extended this process further still, making it possible to map the entire surface of the earth with a degree of precision and completeness that would have seemed fantastical to any cartographer working even a century earlier.

Geographic information systems, which emerged as a practical technology in the 1960s and 1970s and became widely accessible from the 1990s onward, represented a further transformation of equal or greater significance. A geographic information system is not simply a way of storing and displaying maps digitally. It is a way of linking geographical location to arbitrary quantities of associated data, so that a map can become not just a representation of the physical world but a dynamic interface for the exploration of any kind of information that has a spatial dimension. The applications of this technology range from urban planning and environmental monitoring to epidemiology and electoral analysis, and the widespread availability of GIS software and satellite data has democratised cartographic production to a degree that would have been unimaginable in the era of professional monopoly on map-making that persisted through most of the twentieth century.

Today almost everyone in the developed world carries in their pocket a device capable of displaying their precise location on a detailed map of wherever they happen to be, and of providing turn-by-turn navigation instructions to any destination they choose to enter. The technology that makes this possible rests on a layered stack of innovations, from the mathematics of Ptolemy and Mercator to the satellite positioning systems maintained by governments at enormous expense, to the crowdsourced geographical databases built by millions of volunteer contributors, to the rendering algorithms that convert raw geographical data into the familiar visual language of the digital map. Each layer of this stack embeds assumptions and choices that shape what users see and what they do not see, which places are named and which are unnamed, which routes are offered and which are not, whose geographical knowledge counts as authoritative and whose does not.

The cartographic choices embedded in the systems that most people use without thinking about them are not neutral. They reflect the priorities of the organisations that built the systems, the data sources those organisations chose to rely on, the demographic characteristics of the users whose preferences shaped the product design, and the regulatory and legal environments within which the systems operate. A map of the same city can look dramatically different depending on which cartographic choices have been made, and those differences have real consequences for the people who use the maps and for the people whose lives and communities the maps represent. The history of cartography is in this sense not a history that ended when the world was fully surveyed and digitised. It is a history that continues in every decision made by every person who draws a line, assigns a name, or chooses what to leave off the map, which is to say it is a history that continues to be made every day by many thousands of people whose work most of us encounter without knowing it.

Understanding cartography as a practice, rather than simply using maps as tools, requires cultivating a habit of asking who made this map, for what purpose, with what data, according to what choices, and in whose interest. These are not questions that undermine the usefulness of maps. They are questions that make it possible to use maps well, which means using them with an accurate understanding of what they can and cannot reliably show, and with sufficient critical awareness to recognise when the map in front of you is serving someone else's interests rather than your own. The best maps are honest about their own limitations. The worst maps are confident about things they have no right to be confident about. Learning to tell the difference is one of the most practically useful intellectual skills that a person can develop in a world that is increasingly organised around geographical data.

The role of cartography in warfare has been a consistent thread throughout its history, and one that has driven many of the most significant technical advances in the field. Military commanders have always needed to understand the terrain over which their forces would move and fight, and the maps available to them have always been both essential and imperfect. The topographic mapping programmes that most national governments undertook during the nineteenth century were motivated in large part by military necessity: a government that possessed accurate, detailed maps of its own territory was better placed to defend it, and a government that possessed accurate maps of a neighbouring territory was better placed to threaten it. The Ordnance Survey of Great Britain, which began as a direct response to the military threat posed by revolutionary France, is perhaps the best-known example of this dynamic, but equivalent programmes were undertaken in virtually every European state and in many of their colonies during the same period. The institutional frameworks and technical standards developed in these military mapping programmes formed the basis of civilian cartography in most countries for the following century, and the tradition of rigorous field survey and systematic coverage that they established continues to influence national mapping agencies in the twenty-first century, even as the technologies of data collection have changed beyond recognition.

Thematic mapping, which uses geographical frameworks to represent data about phenomena other than physical terrain, emerged as a distinct form of cartographic practice during the nineteenth century and has become one of the most important tools of quantitative social science. John Snow's famous map of cholera cases in the Soho district of London in 1854, which revealed the spatial clustering of cases around a contaminated water pump and helped establish the germ theory of disease transmission, is perhaps the most celebrated early example of thematic mapping as a tool of epidemiological analysis. Charles Joseph Minard's flow map of Napoleon's Russian campaign of 1812, which represents the diminishing strength of the French army against the background of geography, temperature, and time, is widely regarded as one of the most elegant and informative statistical graphics ever produced. These nineteenth-century examples established patterns of thinking about the relationship between geographical space and social data that continue to structure the practice of data visualisation in the twenty-first century, even when the maps in question are interactive, dynamic, and drawn from datasets of a size and complexity that their nineteenth-century precursors could not have imagined. The transition from static printed thematic maps to interactive digital ones has not changed the fundamental intellectual challenge of thematic cartography, which is always the challenge of deciding which aspects of a complex reality to represent and which to simplify or omit, a challenge that is as much editorial and ethical as it is technical.

The Islamic cartographic tradition represents one of the most significant bodies of geographical knowledge produced in the medieval world, and it is one that European histories of cartography have historically undervalued and misrepresented. Scholars working in the Abbasid caliphate and its successor states produced works of geographical synthesis and original observation that had no parallel in the contemporary Latin-speaking world. Al-Idrisi, working in the twelfth century court of Roger II of Sicily, produced a world map and accompanying geographical compendium that drew on centuries of accumulated Islamic geographical scholarship as well as on the direct testimony of travellers and merchants who had reached places that no Latin European had visited. His map, oriented with south at the top in accordance with the convention of the Arabic geographical tradition, depicted the world with a comprehensiveness and accuracy that would not be matched in Latin cartography for several centuries. The fact that this achievement occurred in the intellectual environment of a Norman court in Sicily, at the intersection of Islamic, Byzantine, and Latin Christian cultures, is itself a reminder that the history of cartography, like the history of most intellectual achievements, is a history of cultural exchange as much as it is a history of individual genius or national tradition.
